Battle of Gettysburg

The Turning Point of the Civil War

Presentation Overview

Sponsored by Global Educational Tours, this interactive presentation explores the Battle of Gettysburg—the decisive three-day engagement that marked the turning point of the American Civil War. We'll examine the strategies, key moments, leadership decisions, and profound human cost of this pivotal battle that ended Confederate hopes for Northern invasion.

Student Roles

1. Strategic Context Analyst

Introduction (Hook):

"Why did Robert E. Lee risk his entire army on a second invasion of Northern territory just two months after his greatest victory?"

Content:
Post-Chancellorsville Confidence

After his stunning victory at Chancellorsville (May 2-4, 1863), Lee divided his Army of Northern Virginia into three corps commanded by:

James Longstreet - First Corps
Richard S. Ewell - Second Corps (replacing Stonewall Jackson)
A.P. Hill - Third Corps

This reorganization followed Stonewall Jackson's death after Chancellorsville.

Lee's Strategic Objectives:

• Relieve pressure on war-torn Virginia
• Threaten Northern cities to demoralize Union population
• Win a decisive victory on Northern soil
• Potentially force European recognition of the Confederacy
• Encourage Northern peace movements

The Invasion Route

Lee's army crossed the Blue Ridge Mountains, moved up the Shenandoah Valley, crossed Maryland, and entered Pennsylvania. The campaign aimed to live off Northern resources while drawing the Union army into battle under favorable conditions.

"The enemy is there, and I am going to attack him there." - Robert E. Lee, upon learning Union forces were concentrated at Gettysburg
Conclusion:

"Lee's decision to invade Pennsylvania represented both strategic calculation and overconfidence—a gamble that the Army of Northern Virginia could win any battle, anywhere, despite being outnumbered and deep in enemy territory."

2. Day 1 Chronologist

Introduction (Hook):

"How did a chance encounter between searching Confederates and waiting Union troops escalate into the largest battle ever fought in North America?"

Content:
June 30 - The Prelude

Confederate troops searching for supplies spotted Union cavalry under General John Buford positioned west of Gettysburg. Both sides realized the strategic importance of the road network converging at this small Pennsylvania town.

July 1 - The Battle Begins

The battle opened with an encounter between A.P. Hill's Confederate corps and Union cavalry under General Buford, who dismounted his troopers to fight as infantry while buying time for Union reinforcements.

Key Events - Day 1:

• First Shot: Fired by Lt. Marcellus Jones of the 8th Illinois Cavalry at a mounted Confederate officer
• Union General John Reynolds killed while directing troops
• Confederates forced Union troops back through town to defensive positions on Cemetery Hill and Culp's Hill
• Over 4,000 Union soldiers captured during retreat through Gettysburg
• General James Archer became first Confederate general captured since Lee took command

End of Day 1

By evening, Confederates held the town but Union forces established strong defensive positions on the high ground south of Gettysburg—Cemetery Hill, Culp's Hill, and Cemetery Ridge. This "fishhook" formation would prove crucial in the coming days.

Conclusion:

"The first day's fighting established the battle lines that would define the next two days—with Union forces securing the defensive high ground that Confederate attacks would repeatedly fail to dislodge."

3. Day 2 Tactician

Introduction (Hook):

"How did Union defense and Confederate coordination failures on the second day determine the battle's outcome before Pickett's famous charge?"

Content:
Union Defensive Positions

General Meade arranged his Army of the Potomac in a strong "fishhook" formation:
• Right flank: Culp's Hill
• Center: Cemetery Ridge
• Left flank: Little Round Top and Round Top
This position allowed interior lines and rapid troop movement between threatened sectors.

Lee's Flanking Plan

Lee ordered coordinated attacks on both Union flanks:
• Ewell to attack Culp's Hill and Cemetery Hill
• Longstreet to attack the Union left flank
• Hill to maintain center and support where needed
However, the attacks were poorly coordinated and delayed.

Key Battles - Day 2:

• Little Round Top: Colonel Joshua Chamberlain's 20th Maine held the extreme left flank in desperate fighting
• Peach Orchard, Wheatfield, Devil's Den: Fierce fighting as Longstreet's corps attacked Union positions
• Culp's Hill: Confederates captured part of the hill but couldn't break Union lines
• Longstreet's Delay: His late attack allowed Union reinforcements to strengthen positions

Evening Council of War

Despite heavy losses and pressure on both flanks, Meade's lines held. At an evening council, Union commanders voted unanimously to stay and fight rather than retreat—a crucial decision that set the stage for the third day.

Conclusion:

"The second day's fighting demonstrated both the strength of Union defensive positions and the coordination problems that would doom Confederate efforts—foreshadowing the failure of Lee's strategy even before the famous charge on the third day."

4. Day 3 and Legacy Expert

Introduction (Hook):

"Why did Lee order Pickett's Charge against overwhelming odds, and how did its failure transform the entire Civil War?"

Content:
Pickett's Charge

On July 3, Lee ordered a massive assault on the Union center on Cemetery Ridge:
• Approximately 12,500 Confederate soldiers across three divisions
• Preceded by the largest artillery bombardment of the war
• Attack delayed until afternoon due to coordination issues
• Faced devastating Union artillery and rifle fire across open ground

The Charge and Repulse:

• Confederates briefly breached Union lines at the "Angle"
• Union reinforcements rushed to plug the gap
• Attackers suffered over 50% casualties
• Only a few hundred Confederates reached Union lines
• The attack collapsed in less than an hour

Aftermath and Retreat

With Pickett's Charge repulsed, the battle effectively ended. On July 4, Lee began his retreat to Virginia, expecting a counterattack that never came due to heavy rains and Union exhaustion. The Army of Northern Virginia had suffered losses from which it would never fully recover.

Army Killed Wounded Missing/Captured Total % of Force
Union 3,155 14,530 5,365 23,040 27%
Confederate 3,903* 18,735* 5,425* 28,063* 39%*

*Estimated figures - Confederate records were less complete

Historical Significance

Gettysburg marked:
• The end of Confederate offensive operations in the North
• The failure of Lee's invasion strategy
• A psychological turning point in the war
• The beginning of the end for the Confederacy
• The site of Lincoln's Gettysburg Address months later

Conclusion:

"Pickett's Charge represented the high-water mark of the Confederacy—both literally and symbolically. Its failure ensured that Gettysburg would be remembered not just as a battle loss, but as the turning point that doomed the Confederate cause and preserved the United States."

Tragically, General John Reynolds was killed directing reinforcements. Confederates drove Union forces through the town, capturing over 4,000 soldiers, but the Union established strong defensive positions on Cemetery Hill, Culp's Hill, and Cemetery Ridge—the 'fishhook' formation that would prove crucial. By day's end, Confederates held the town but faced Union troops on the high ground."

Day 2 Tactician

"On July 2, Meade arranged his forces in a strong defensive 'fishhook' from Culp's Hill to Little Round Top. Lee planned coordinated attacks on both Union flanks, but poor coordination and delays plagued Confederate efforts. Longstreet's late attack on the Union left led to fierce fighting at Little Round Top (where Chamberlain's 20th Maine held the flank), Devil's Den, the Wheatfield, and Peach Orchard. On the right, Ewell attacked Culp's Hill but only captured part of it. Despite heavy pressure, Union lines held. At an evening council, Meade's commanders unanimously voted to stay and fight—a crucial decision that set the stage for the third day's dramatic events."

Day 3 and Legacy Expert

"On July 3, Lee ordered Pickett's Charge—a massive assault by 12,500 Confederates against the Union center on Cemetery Ridge. After the largest artillery bombardment of the war, the infantry advanced across open ground into devastating Union fire. Though briefly penetrating Union lines at the 'Angle,' the attack collapsed with over 50% casualties. This repulse marked the 'high-water mark of the Confederacy.' Lee retreated on July 4, his army never to recover fully. The battle cost approximately 51,000 total casualties, making it the bloodiest of the war. Gettysburg ended Confederate offensive operations in the North and marked the turning point of the Civil War, setting the stage for ultimate Union victory."

Interactive Elements

Audience Questions

Gettysburg is considered the turning point because it ended Confederate offensive operations in the North, destroyed Lee's aura of invincibility, and caused casualties from which the Army of Northern Virginia never fully recovered. Combined with the fall of Vicksburg the next day, it reversed the war's momentum permanently in favor of the Union. The defeat ended Confederate hopes for European recognition and forced Lee to fight a defensive war that ultimately led to defeat due to the North's superior resources and manpower.

Most historians identify several key mistakes: (1) Ordering Pickett's Charge against entrenched positions across open ground; (2) Failing to coordinate attacks properly on July 2; (3) Attacking the Union army while outnumbered and in strong defensive positions; (4) Dispensing with cavalry (J.E.B. Stuart was absent gathering intelligence) which left him blind to Union positions; and (5) Underestimating the Army of the Potomac's fighting ability after Chancellorsville. Lee's characteristic aggressiveness, which served him well previously, proved disastrous at Gettysburg.

Meade didn't pursue Lee aggressively for several reasons: (1) The Army of the Potomac had suffered nearly 23,000 casualties and was exhausted; (2) Heavy rains made roads impassable and hindered movement; (3) Meade needed to reorganize his battered army; (4) He feared a Confederate counterattack if he advanced recklessly; (5) The Union command structure was still consolidating after Meade had only recently taken command. While Lincoln was frustrated by the lack of pursuit, most military historians believe Meade's caution was reasonable given his army's condition and the need to preserve this hard-won victory.
