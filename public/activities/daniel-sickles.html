The Scandalous and Heroic Tale of General Daniel Sickles

A Washington, D.C. and Gettysburg Adventure

Introduction: A Larger-Than-Life Figure

For students and educators embarking on a Washington, D.C. or Gettysburg school trip with Global Educational Tours, the story of Major General Daniel Edgar Sickles offers a captivating glimpse into a man whose life was as controversial as it was consequential. A New York politician, a Union general, and a central figure in one of America's most infamous legal cases, Sickles' journey weaves together scandal, bravery, and a touch of the bizarre.

From the streets of Washington, D.C., where he made history with a shocking murder trial, to the battlefields of Gettysburg, where his defiance may have altered the course of the Civil War, Sickles' story is one that students will find both educational and unforgettable. Let's dive into the life of this complex figure, exploring his political career, his notorious trial, his military exploits, and the curious legacy of his amputated leg, now displayed at the National Museum of Health and Medicine.

The Political Rise of Daniel Sickles

Born on October 20, 1819, in New York City (though some sources suggest 1825, as Sickles liked to obscure his age), Daniel Sickles was a product of the Tammany Hall political machine, a powerful Democratic organization. His early career was marked by ambition and connections.

After studying law and being admitted to the bar in 1846, Sickles quickly rose through New York's political ranks. By 1847, he was a member of the New York State Assembly, and in 1853, he served as corporation counsel for New York City, playing a key role in acquiring the land for Central Park. His political ascent continued when he joined the U.S. legation in London as secretary under James Buchanan, appointed by President Franklin Pierce. Returning to the U.S., Sickles served in the New York State Senate (1856–1857) and was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives as a Democrat, serving from 1857 to 1861.

Sickles and his young wife, Teresa Bagioli, lived a lavish lifestyle in Washington, D.C., leasing a mansion in Lafayette Square, just across from the White House. However, Sickles' personal life was far from pristine. Known for his own extramarital affairs, he was no stranger to scandal—but nothing prepared the nation for the drama that unfolded in 1859.

A Scandal in Lafayette Square: The Murder of Philip Barton Key

On February 27, 1859, Sickles' life took a dramatic turn when he discovered that his wife, Teresa, was having an affair with Philip Barton Key II, the U.S. Attorney for the District of Columbia and son of Francis Scott Key, the composer of "The Star-Spangled Banner." Enraged, Sickles confronted Key in broad daylight in Lafayette Square, shouting, "Key, you scoundrel, you have dishonored my house—you must die!" He then shot and killed Key. The murder, committed steps from the White House, sent shockwaves through Washington society.

Sickles surrendered to authorities and was jailed, but his political connections ensured preferential treatment—he was even allowed to keep his personal weapon and receive high-profile visitors. His defense team, led by future Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton, made history by employing the first successful use of the "temporary insanity" defense in U.S. legal history. Arguing that Sickles was driven temporarily mad by his wife's infidelity, the team secured his acquittal on April 26, 1859.

However, Sickles further outraged the public by reconciling with Teresa, a decision that left him socially ostracized, as one diarist noted, "as if he had the smallpox." This scandal, unfolding in the heart of Washington, D.C., is a gripping tale for students exploring the capital's history, highlighting the intersection of law, politics, and personal drama.

From Scandal to the Battlefield: Sickles' Military Career

With his reputation in tatters, Sickles saw the outbreak of the Civil War in 1861 as a chance for redemption. Despite lacking military experience, his political clout secured him a commission as colonel of the 70th New York Infantry, part of the Excelsior Brigade he helped raise. By May 1862, he was promoted to brigadier general, and in November 1862, he became a major general, commanding the III Corps of the Army of the Potomac. His bravery in battles like Seven Pines and the Seven Days Battles was undeniable, but his tendency to clash with superiors foreshadowed the controversy that would define his role at Gettysburg.

The Battle of Gettysburg: A Costly Decision and a Lost Leg

On July 2, 1863, during the second day of the Battle of Gettysburg, Sickles' actions became one of the most debated moments of the Civil War. Ordered by Major General George G. Meade to position his III Corps along Cemetery Ridge, anchoring the Union left flank to Little Round Top, Sickles instead moved his 12,000-man corps nearly a mile forward to the Peach Orchard, believing its higher ground offered a tactical advantage. This unauthorized move created a salient in the Union line, exposing his troops to attack from multiple sides.

When Confederate Lieutenant General James Longstreet's corps launched a ferocious assault, Sickles' III Corps was overwhelmed, suffering 40% casualties and rendering it combat-ineffective. During the fighting, a 12-pound cannonball shattered Sickles' right leg. Refusing to let morale falter, he was carried from the field on a stretcher, puffing a cigar to inspire his troops. His leg was amputated that afternoon, and he was evacuated to Washington, D.C., arriving on July 4 with some of the first news of the Union victory.

Historians remain divided on Sickles' decision. Some, like Edwin B. Coddington, blame him for nearly causing a Union disaster, while others, like John Keegan and James M. McPherson, argue his move disrupted Longstreet's plan, potentially saving the Union line from collapse. For students visiting Gettysburg with Global Educational Tours, Sickles' story adds depth to the battlefield experience, illustrating the high stakes of leadership and the consequences of defying orders.

The Legacy of a Leg: The National Museum of Health and Medicine

Perhaps the most bizarre chapter of Sickles' story is what happened to his amputated leg. Aware of a new directive from the Army Surgeon General to collect "specimens of morbid anatomy," Sickles donated the bones of his shattered leg to the Army Medical Museum (now the National Museum of Health and Medicine) in Washington, D.C. Accompanied by a card reading, "With the compliments of Major General D.E.S.," the tibia and fibula were preserved in a small coffin-shaped box.

For years, Sickles reportedly visited his leg on the anniversary of his injury, July 2, even berating the museum for not preserving his foot as well. Today, the leg remains on display, alongside other Civil War-era artifacts like the hip of General Henry Barnum, making it a must-see for students on Washington, D.C. school trips interested in the intersection of history and medical science.

A Long and Colorful Life

Sickles' military career ended at Gettysburg, but his public life continued to flourish. After the war, he served as military governor of the Carolinas (1865–1867), pushing for fair treatment of freed slaves, though his performance led to his removal by President Andrew Johnson. He retired from the army in 1869 as a major general and served as U.S. Minister to Spain (1869–1873), where he was rumored to have had an affair with the deposed Queen Isabella II.

Back in the U.S., he served as sheriff of New York County (1890), returned to Congress (1893–1895), and played a pivotal role in establishing Gettysburg National Military Park through legislation passed in 1895. His efforts helped preserve the battlefield for future generations, a legacy students can appreciate on their Gettysburg tours.

Despite financial scandals, including his removal from the New York State Monuments Commission in 1912 for alleged fund misuse, Sickles remained a larger-than-life figure. He died on May 3, 1914, at age 94, in New York City, and was buried with full military honors at Arlington National Cemetery. In 1897, he was awarded the Medal of Honor for his actions at Gettysburg, cited for "most conspicuous gallantry" despite his controversial decisions.

Conclusion: A Man of Contradictions

Daniel Sickles' life is a study in contradictions: a politician who thrived on scandal, a general who defied orders yet earned a Medal of Honor, and a man who turned a gruesome injury into a peculiar legacy. For students and educators exploring Washington, D.C. and Gettysburg with Global Educational Tours, Sickles' story offers a lens into the complexities of American history.

From the drama of his trial in Lafayette Square to his fateful decision at the Peach Orchard and the enduring display of his leg at the National Museum of Health and Medicine, Sickles embodies the audacity, flaws, and resilience of a nation at war with itself. As you walk the streets of D.C. or the fields of Gettysburg, let Sickles' tale remind you that history is shaped not just by heroes, but by those who dare to defy convention—sometimes at great cost, and sometimes with unexpected valor.
