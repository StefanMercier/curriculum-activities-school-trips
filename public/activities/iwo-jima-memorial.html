US Marine Corps War Memorial

Iwo Jima Memorial

Presentation Overview

Sponsored by Global Educational Tours, this interactive presentation explores the US Marine Corps War Memorial, commonly known as the Iwo Jima Memorial. We'll examine the historic battle that inspired it, the famous photograph that captured a pivotal moment, the creation of the memorial, and the complex story behind the men depicted in this iconic symbol of American valor.

Student Roles

1. Battle of Iwo Jima Historian

Introduction (Hook):

"Why was a tiny island two-thirds the size of Manhattan so strategically important that it became the site of one of World War II's bloodiest battles?"

Content:
  • Iwo Jima was a small volcanic island 660 miles south of Tokyo
  • Strategic importance: contained airfields crucial for bombing campaigns against mainland Japan
  • First time foreign forces occupied Japanese territory in nearly 500 years
  • The battle lasted from February 19 to March 26, 1945
  • Extremely costly: 6,821 Americans killed, 19,217 wounded
  • Almost all 21,000 Japanese defenders fought to death or committed suicide
  • The capture of Mount Suribachi was a key objective
Strategic Significance:

"Iwo Jima provided emergency landing fields for damaged B-29 bombers returning from missions over Japan. The island's capture saved approximately 2,400 airmen and 24,000 aircrew members who would otherwise have crashed at sea."

Conclusion:

"The brutal Battle of Iwo Jima, while extraordinarily costly in human lives, provided a crucial strategic advantage that ultimately helped bring the Pacific War to a conclusion and saved thousands of American airmen."

2. Photography and Iconography Expert

Introduction (Hook):

"How did one photograph become perhaps the most iconic image of World War II and inspire a national monument?"

Content:
Joe Rosenthal's Photograph

On February 23, 1945, Associated Press photographer Joe Rosenthal captured the second flag raising on Mount Suribachi. The image shows six soldiers raising a larger replacement flag to ensure it would be visible across the island.

The Two Flag Raisings:

• First Flag Raising: A small flag was raised around 10:30 AM to signal the capture of Mount Suribachi

• Second Flag Raising: A larger flag was raised around 12:35 PM to ensure visibility across the island

• Rosenthal's famous photo captured the second raising

• The photo was initially mistaken for the first raising

  • The photograph was transmitted to the US and published nationwide
  • It immediately became a symbol of American determination and victory
  • Won the Pulitzer Prize in 1945
  • Inspired a resurgence of national pride and support for war bonds
  • Three of the six men in the photo died in subsequent fighting on Iwo Jima
Conclusion:

"Rosenthal's photograph captured not just a military action, but the spirit of American resilience and sacrifice, instantly becoming an iconic symbol that would eventually be immortalized in bronze."

3. Memorial Creation Specialist

Introduction (Hook):

"How did a photograph become the world's largest cast bronze statue, honoring all Marines who have died in service since 1775?"

Content:
Creating the Memorial

Sculptor Felix de Weldon was so moved by Rosenthal's photograph that he created a maquette (small model) within 48 hours of seeing it. This eventually led to the creation of the full-scale memorial.

32-foot tall figures
60-foot bronze flagpole
100-ton memorial weight
78-foot overall height
9 years to complete
Dedicated November 10, 1954
  • Dedicated by President Dwight D. Eisenhower
  • Inscriptions list every major Marine Corps action since 1775
  • The base bears the inscription: "Uncommon Valor Was a Common Virtue"
  • Also inscribed: "In honor and memory of the men of the United States Marine Corps who have given their lives to their country since November 10, 1775"
  • Three survivors of the flag raising attended the dedication
Conclusion:

"The memorial transforms a momentary act of victory into an eternal tribute to Marine Corps sacrifice, honoring all Marines who have died in service throughout American history."

4. Flag Raisers' Storyteller

Introduction (Hook):

"The story behind the famous photograph involves confusion, tragedy, and a seven-decade-long mystery about the identities of the men depicted."

Content:
The Six Flag Raisers

For 71 years, it was believed the six men in the photo were:

  • Ira Hayes (Pima Indian)
  • John Bradley (Navy Corpsman)
  • Rene Gagnon
  • Michael Strank (killed March 1)
  • Franklin Sousley (killed March 21)
  • Harlon Block (killed March 1)
The Bond Tour Controversy:

After the photo's publication, the three survivors were ordered to return to the US for a war bond tour. However: • Ira Hayes resisted publicity and didn't want to be identified • Rene Gagnon was forced to identify Hayes • Confusion existed because there were two flag raisings • John Bradley rarely spoke about his experience

The 2016 Revelation

In 2016, historical research revealed that: • John Bradley was in the first flag raising, not the second • Harold Schultz was actually the sixth man in the famous photo • This was determined through photographic analysis of uniforms, equipment, and body positions • The discovery came 71 years after the event

"The true story behind the photo is more complex than the legend, but no less heroic. All who fought on Iwo Jima displayed extraordinary courage." - James Bradley, son of John Bradley and author of "Flags of Our Fathers"
Conclusion:

"The evolving understanding of the flag raisers' identities reminds us that history is often more complex than legend, but the collective courage and sacrifice of all who fought at Iwo Jima remains undiminished."

Printable Student Scripts

Battle of Iwo Jima Historian

"The Battle of Iwo Jima was fought from February 19 to March 26, 1945, on a small volcanic island just 660 miles from Tokyo. Though only two-thirds the size of Manhattan, Iwo Jima was strategically crucial because its airfields could support B-29 bombing campaigns against mainland Japan. The battle was extraordinarily costly: 6,821 Americans were killed and 19,217 wounded, while nearly all 21,000 Japanese defenders fought to the death. The capture of Mount Suribachi on February 23rd marked a turning point, with the famous flag raising signaling the first time foreign forces had occupied Japanese territory in nearly 500 years."

Photography and Iconography Expert

"Associated Press photographer Joe Rosenthal captured the iconic flag raising image on February 23, 1945, during the second flag raising on Mount Suribachi. The Marines raised a larger replacement flag to ensure visibility across the island. Rosenthal's photograph was transmitted to the US where it immediately became a symbol of American determination, winning the Pulitzer Prize and inspiring increased support for the war effort through bond drives. Tragically, three of the six men in the photograph—Michael Strank, Franklin Sousley, and Harlon Block—were killed in subsequent fighting on Iwo Jima before the photo was published."

Memorial Creation Specialist

"Sculptor Felix de Weldon created the US Marine Corps War Memorial after being deeply moved by Rosenthal's photograph. He made a small model within 48 hours of seeing the image, which eventually led to the creation of the massive bronze memorial. Dedicated in 1954 by President Eisenhower, the memorial features 32-foot-tall figures raising a 60-foot flagpole, making it the largest cast bronze statue in the world. The base lists every major Marine Corps action since 1775 and bears the inscription 'Uncommon Valor Was a Common Virtue,' honoring all Marines who have died in service to their country."

Flag Raisers' Storyteller

"For 71 years, it was believed the six flag raisers were Ira Hayes, John Bradley, Rene Gagnon, Michael Strank, Franklin Sousley, and Harlon Block. The three survivors were ordered home for a war bond tour, though Ira Hayes resisted the publicity. In 2016, research revealed that John Bradley was actually in the first flag raising, not the second photographed by Rosenthal. Harold Schultz was identified as the sixth man through analysis of uniforms and equipment. This discovery, detailed in James Bradley's book 'Flags of Our Fathers,' shows history is often more complex than legend, but doesn't diminish the courage of all who fought at Iwo Jima."

Audience Questions

Iwo Jima was crucial because its airfields were located just 660 miles from Tokyo, making them ideal for supporting B-29 bombing campaigns against mainland Japan. The island provided emergency landing fields for damaged bombers returning from missions, ultimately saving approximately 2,400 airmen and 24,000 aircrew members who would otherwise have crashed at sea. Its capture marked the first time foreign forces had occupied Japanese territory in nearly 500 years.

There were two flag raisings on Mount Suribachi, and Rosenthal's famous photo captured the second one. This led to initial confusion about which raising was photographed. Later, there was controversy about the identities of the men—for 71 years, it was believed Navy Corpsman John Bradley was in the photo, but research in 2016 revealed he was actually in the first raising, and Harold Schultz was the sixth man in the famous second raising. Additionally, the bond tour that followed created tension, with Ira Hayes resisting publicity.

The US Marine Corps War Memorial is the largest cast bronze statue in the world. It features 32-foot-tall figures raising a 60-foot bronze flagpole, with the entire memorial standing 78 feet tall and weighing 100 tons. It took sculptor Felix de Weldon nine years to complete. The memorial is unique in that it honors all Marines who have died in service since 1775, with inscriptions listing every major Marine Corps action throughout American history.
