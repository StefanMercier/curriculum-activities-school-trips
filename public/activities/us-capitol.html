Student Roles

1. Historical Context Guide

Introduction (Hook):

"After the American Revolution, the new nation needed a capital—but where should it be located? The decision would require one of the first major political compromises in U.S. history."

Content:
  • The Residence Act of 1790 and the compromise between Hamilton, Jefferson, and Madison
  • Why the Potomac River location was selected
  • George Washington's personal role in choosing the exact site
  • The original 10×10 mile diamond shape and boundary stones
  • The meaning of "D.C." - District of Columbia
Conclusion:

"This political compromise didn't just determine where our capital would be—it established a symbol of national unity that would become a beacon of democracy worldwide."

2. City Design Expert

Introduction (Hook):

"A French-born artist with no formal training in urban planning designed one of the world's most recognizable cities—before cars even existed!"

Content:
  • Pierre Charles L'Enfant's background and vision
  • The symbolic placement of the Capitol at the center
  • The grid system with lettered and numbered streets
  • Diagonal avenues named after states
  • Traffic circles and their original purpose
  • Benjamin Banneker's role in preserving the plans

Conclusion:

"L'Enfant's brilliant design created not just a functional city but a symbolic representation of American democracy that continues to function over 200 years later."

3. Geography & Layout Specialist

Introduction (Hook):

"Washington's layout seems confusing at first, but it's actually a brilliantly simple system that makes navigation surprisingly easy once you understand it."

Content:
  • The four quadrants (NW, NE, SW, SE) centered on the Capitol
  • Numbered streets running north-south
  • Lettered streets running east-west (A-W, skipping J)
  • State-named diagonal avenues
  • How addresses indicate quadrant and location
  • Key landmarks and their symbolic placement
Conclusion:

"D.C.'s logical design reflects the Enlightenment values of order and reason that inspired the nation's founders, creating a city that's both functional and symbolic."

4. Modern D.C. Guide

Introduction (Hook):

"Beyond the monuments and government buildings, Washington, D.C. is a vibrant city with unique neighborhoods, a diverse population, and a complex political status."

Content:
  • D.C.'s unique status as a federal district, not a state
  • The "Taxation Without Representation" license plates
  • Famous neighborhoods and their characteristics
  • The role of the District's non-voting Congressional delegate
  • Cultural institutions beyond the National Mall
  • Demographics and diversity of the city
Conclusion:

"Washington, D.C. is more than just the seat of government—it's a dynamic city with its own identity, culture, and unique place in American democracy."

Printable Student Scripts

Historical Context Guide

"After experiencing the weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation, the Founding Fathers knew the new nation needed a permanent capital. The Residence Act of 1790 resulted from a famous compromise: Alexander Hamilton supported a southern Potomac location in exchange for southern support for his financial plan. President Washington personally selected the exact site—just 14 miles upstream from his Mount Vernon estate."

City Design Expert

"Pierre Charles L'Enfant envisioned a capital that would rival European cities in grandeur while symbolizing American ideals. He placed the Capitol building on Jenkins Hill, making it the physical center of the city. From there, he designed a grid of streets intersected by diagonal avenues named after states, creating circles and squares that would serve as neighborhood centers. His vision was so grand that he was eventually dismissed for exceeding his budget and authority."

Geography & Layout Specialist

"D.C. is divided into four quadrants centered on the Capitol building. Numbered streets run north-south, and lettered streets run east-west (skipping J). State-named diagonal avenues create the circles and squares. Addresses include the quadrant (e.g., 1600 Pennsylvania Ave NW), making navigation straightforward. The system was designed before automobiles but works perfectly even with modern traffic patterns."

Modern D.C. Guide

"Today, D.C. is home to over 700,000 residents who pay federal taxes but lack voting representation in Congress. The city's 'Taxation Without Representation' license plates highlight this unique status. Beyond government, D.C. boasts diverse neighborhoods from historic Georgetown to vibrant Adams Morgan, a thriving arts scene, and some of the nation's best museums—most offering free admission to all visitors."

Audience Questions

District of Columbia. Columbia was a poetic name for America honoring Christopher Columbus, used before "United States" became standard.

It was a compromise between northern and southern states, and George Washington believed the area had commercial potential as a shipping center if connected to the western frontier by canal.

The city is divided into Northwest (NW), Northeast (NE), Southwest (SW), and Southeast (SE) quadrants centered on the U.S. Capitol building. Addresses include the quadrant abbreviation.
