The Freedom Trail, Boston

Walking Through American History

Presentation Overview

Sponsored by Global Educational Tours, this interactive presentation explores Boston's Freedom Trail—the 2.5-mile red-brick path that connects 16 historically significant sites. We'll follow the trail from Boston Common to Charlestown, exploring the people, places, and events that shaped the American Revolution and the nation's founding.

Part 1: Boston Common to Granary Burying Ground
Part 2: King's Chapel to Old South Meeting House
Part 3: Old State House to Faneuil Hall
Part 4: Paul Revere House to USS Constitution
Freedom Trail Quick Facts
Length: 2.5 miles
Sites: 16 historic locations
Established: 1951
Start: Boston Common
End: USS Constitution
Marker: Red brick/ paint line

Student Roles

1. Trail Beginnings Guide

Introduction (Hook):

"How does a simple red line connect the most important sites of the American Revolution through the heart of modern Boston?"

Content:
Boston Common

Oldest public park in North America (1634). Originally owned by William Blackstone before Puritan arrival. Served as common grazing ground, military training field, and site of public punishments. Starting point of the Freedom Trail.

Massachusetts State House

Legislative assembly since 1795. Designed by Charles Bulfinch. Golden dome originally made of wood, later covered with copper by Paul Revere, then gilded with 23-karat gold. Faces the wealthy Beacon Hill district.

Shaw 54th Regiment Memorial

Honors the first all-volunteer black regiment from the North during Civil War. Led by Colonel Robert Gould Shaw who welcomed black Massachusetts residents to fight for Union cause and free enslaved people in the South.

Park Street Church ("Brimstone Church")

Founded 1809. Nicknamed "Brimstone Church" for its fiery abolitionist and evangelical sermons. Site where "America" (My Country 'Tis of Thee) was first sung in 1831.

Granary Burying Ground

Contains tombs of revolutionary leaders: James Otis (first patriotic leader), Samuel Adams (revolution organizer), and cenotaph of Benjamin Franklin's parents (Josiah and Abiah). Benjamin Franklin buried in Philadelphia.

Conclusion:

"The first section of the Freedom Trail introduces the foundations of Boston's revolutionary spirit—from the common land that symbolized shared community to the burial ground holding the patriots who dared to imagine independence."

2. Colonial Center Interpreter

Introduction (Hook):

"How did religious, educational, and governmental institutions in colonial Boston become centers of revolutionary thought and action?"

Content:
King's Chapel

Anglican church symbolizing controversial British presence on Puritan soil. Nicknamed "Stone Chapel." First Anglican church in New England (1686). Current building (1754) never had a steeple—Puritans refused to contribute to "English church."

First Public School Site/Ben Franklin Statue

Location of Boston Latin School (1635), oldest public school in America. Benjamin Franklin attended but didn't graduate—his statue honors his contributions despite his limited formal education.

Old Corner Bookstore

Center of American literature in 1800s. Housed Ticknor and Fields, most important publisher of the era. Hosted authors like Hawthorne, Longfellow, Dickens, and Stowe.

Old South Meeting House

One of the centers of the American Revolution. Largest building in colonial Boston, hosting crowds to hear famous speakers. Starting point of the Boston Tea Party (1773) after meetings about tea tax protests.

Conclusion:

"Boston's colonial institutions—whether religious, educational, or commercial—became incubators of revolutionary ideas where citizens gathered to debate, educate themselves, and ultimately organize resistance to British rule."

3. Revolutionary Heart Specialist

Introduction (Hook):

"Which buildings witnessed the most dramatic confrontations between colonists and British authorities that ultimately led to revolution?"

Content:
Old State House

Oldest public building in Boston (1713). Served as legislative assembly and center of colonial authority. Site of many confrontations between settlers and British officials. Boston Massacre occurred in front of building (1770)—British soldiers killed five civilians. Now museum with exhibits on revolutionary history.

Faneuil Hall ("Cradle of American Liberty")

Center of political and social life for 250 years. Gift from Huguenot merchant Peter Faneuil (1742). Huguenots were French Protestants persecuted by Louis XIV who fled to America. Building condition: marketplace on first floor, meeting hall on second. Sons of Liberty planned activities against British in secret top-floor meetings. Still functions as marketplace and meeting space today.

Sons of Liberty

Secret patriotic society organized in American colonies. Leaders included Samuel Adams, Paul Revere, and John Hancock. Planned resistance to British policies through protests, boycotts, and eventually revolutionary action.

Conclusion:

"The Old State House and Faneuil Hall formed the revolutionary heart of Boston—where protests turned to confrontation, speeches became action, and the Sons of Liberty transformed philosophical objections into organized resistance."

4. North End and Charlestown Expert

Introduction (Hook):

"How did the North End's streets and Charlestown's heights become the setting for the revolution's most dramatic events and symbols?"

Content:
Paul Revere House

Oldest building in Boston (1680). Home of Paul Revere—silversmith, engraver, and Sons of Liberty leader. Preserved as memorial to his revolutionary activities, including his famous midnight ride.

Old North Church

Christ Church (1723). Tallest building in colonial Boston. Site of famous "one if by land, two if by sea" signal (April 18, 1775). Robert Newman, church sexton, hung two lanterns in steeple to warn that British were coming "by sea" across Charles River. Immortalized in Longfellow's poem "Paul Revere's Ride."

Copp's Hill Burying Ground

Second oldest cemetery in Boston. Final resting place of merchants, artisans, and craftspeople who lived in North End. British used hill to aim cannons at Charlestown during Battle of Bunker Hill.

Charlestown Bridge

Crosses Charles River from salt to fresh water. Leads to Charlestown district and final Freedom Trail sites.

Bunker Hill Monument

Honors Battle of Bunker Hill (actually fought on Breed's Hill, June 17, 1775). Though British technically won, high casualties proved Americans could fight professional soldiers. Crucial moral victory for patriots.

USS Constitution ("Old Ironsides")

US Navy warship launched 1797. Oldest commissioned warship still afloat. Nicknamed "Old Ironsides" because cannonballs seemed to bounce off her strong oak hull. Still manned by active US Navy crew—living naval history.

Conclusion:

"The Freedom Trail's northern sites represent the revolution's dramatic culmination—from the secret signals that launched armed conflict to the battlefields where independence was won and the ships that protected the new nation."

Interactive Elements

Audience Questions

William Blackstone (sometimes spelled Blaxton) owned the land that became Boston Common. He was a Anglican minister and England's first settler in the Boston area, living as a hermit on the peninsula he called Shawmut. When Puritans arrived in 1630 and settled in Charlestown, they faced water shortages. Blackstone invited them to move to his side of the river where spring water was plentiful. The Puritans purchased the land from him, and he moved to Rhode Island.

The Boston Massacre occurred in front of the Old State House on March 5, 1770. British soldiers opened fire on a crowd of colonists, killing five men including Crispus Attucks, an African American considered the first casualty of the American Revolution. The event became a powerful propaganda tool for patriots. Interestingly, future president John Adams defended the British soldiers in court, demonstrating the colonists' commitment to rule of law even amidst revolutionary tension.

During the War of 1812, the USS Constitution earned the nickname "Old Ironsides" when sailors observed British cannonballs seeming to bounce off her thick oak hull. The ship's live oak construction made her exceptionally strong and resistant to enemy fire. The name became famous after Oliver Wendell Holmes' poem "Old Ironsides" helped save the ship from being decommissioned. Today, it remains the world's oldest commissioned naval vessel still afloat, with an active US Navy crew.

King's Chapel was controversial because it was the first Anglican church in Puritan Boston. Puritans had left England to escape Anglican authority, so an Anglican church in their midst represented the authority they had rejected. Puritans refused to contribute to its construction or allow a steeple (which is why it's sometimes called "Stone Chapel"). The church symbolized British religious and political authority in a city founded to escape that very authority.

Park Street Church earned the nickname "Brimstone Church" because of the fiery, hellfire-and-brimstone sermons preached there in the 19th century. The church was known for its strong evangelical and abolitionist stance. It was here that William Lloyd Garrison gave his first anti-slavery speech in 1829, and where the hymn "America" (My Country 'Tis of Thee) was first sung in 1831. The name reflects both the theological content and the passionate social activism that characterized the church.

Activity: Freedom Trail Scavenger Hunt

Have students work in small groups to:

  1. Research different Freedom Trail sites and their significance
  2. Create a map showing the trail's route and key locations
  3. Develop clues or questions about each site
  4. Design a timeline connecting the sites to historical events
  5. Create profiles of key figures associated with each location
  6. Present their scavenger hunt to other groups
  7. Discuss how physical spaces preserve and communicate history
